An Act establishing the Massachusetts Digital Commonwealth. 
 
 Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives in General Court assembled, and by the authority 
of the same, as follows: 
 
1  SECTION 1. Findings and Declarations. 
2  (a) Artificial intelligence is becoming ubiquitous across the Commonwealth. In 
3 particular, the systems are used to impact decisions around housing, employment, healthcare, 
4 education, social development and interactions, and access to public services. The outcomes and 
5 inferences, the process by which a deployed machine learning model or generative AI system 
6 produces outputs, are not transparent to the individuals and residents they affect. It is readily 
7 apparent these systems shape the livelihoods and socioeconomic development of Massachusetts 
8 residents often without their knowledge. 
9  (b) The invisible use of AI systems in consequential decisions around human life, 
10 development, and dignity is the primary risk to an individual's cognitive sovereignty and to 
11 democratic governance itself. These systems have the capability to assess residents without 
12 disclosure, modify an individual's or collective group's experience based on an assessment, and 
13 shape what information they receive, how options or decisions are framed, and what a person 

2 of 58 14 comes to believe is possible. This is drawn from the data collected about them through the 
15 interaction between human and AI systems and characteristics the system inferred from it; all 
16 invisibly and without the individual's knowledge. 
17  (c) Article 89 of the Amendments to the Massachusetts Constitution, specifically the 
18 Home Rule Amendment approved by voters in 1966, and Bloom v. Worcester, 363 Mass. 136 
19 (1973) give municipalities the autonomy to draft and enforce ordinances so as they do not 
20 conflict with State law and do not interfere with the aggregate intent of the legislature, 
21 specifically an intent that courts are permitted to infer even from statutes that do not reference or 
22 mention municipalities at all. 
23  (d) The General Court determines that municipalities possess the constitutional authority 
24 to require transparency around AI systems operating in their communities. This authority exists 
25 whether or not a municipality chooses to exercise it and shall not be taken away silently. Any 
26 restriction on that authority must be stated by the General Court, expressly in plain language, 
27 such that no court reads a statute that never mentions a municipality as taking that authority away 
28 and so that the customary and traditional liberties of the people in the conduct of their local 
29 government are not infringed by silence or invisibility. 
30  (e) The General Court further determines that when any physical AI infrastructure, such 
31 as a data center, operates in a community; the impacts that cross beyond the facility's site 
32 boundaries, including the water it consumes, the demands put on the electric grid, and the 
33 emissions of its backup generation are impacts on the community itself. A municipality shall 
34 have the authority to require the owner or operator of such a facility to make regular and 
35 transparent reports of these impacts. Their reporting must be drawn from readily available data, 

3 of 58 36 expressed in common, explainable units. These reports must be provided to the municipality and 
37 to the residents of the community. This finding does not limit the right of residents to 
38 transparency around the outputs and inferences of any AI system or machine learning algorithm 
39 that operates from such a facility when used in consequential decisions around their lives, as 
40 provided in these findings and this act. The authority vested in the municipality is in addition to, 
41 and does not diminish, any existing authority around land use, zoning, and public health. This 
42 authority shall not be taken away as provided in these findings. 
43  (f) The General Court further determines that when AI systems are deployed and used, 
44 the interactions generate data around the people and communities they operate in. This data is 
45 then collected and retained, and fed back into the training and improving of these models 
46 (creating a feedback loop where deployment creates new data and the data improves the model). 
47 This loop gives the data generated by residents and their communities real economic value to the 
48 owner or operator of said system. The residents of the communities, that generate this data have 
49 the right to know when their information goes back into this loop, whether it is retained, used to 
50 train or improve AI and machine learning systems, or is shared, such that the value taken from a 
51 community is visible to the community itself in accessible and common language. 
52  (g) The General Court further determines that when AI systems are offered to 
53 governments at nominal cost, through federal and state purchasing vehicles, these systems arrive 
54 in the schools, the police departments, sheriffs' offices, and the service offices of a community 
55 faster than a community can deliberate around them. The speed at which they are implemented 
56 reduces their ability to defend their own cognitive sovereignty and, in essence, is a choice made 
57 for them not by them. A municipality shall have the authority to make its own decisions around 
58 the adoption and operation of AI systems in its own operations and services, to evaluate and 

4 of 58 59 adopt them, to decline and discontinue them, and to set their terms of use. This authority belongs 
60 to the self government of the community itself. No State agency, executive order, regulation, or 
61 administrative action shall interfere with it. Any restriction on this authority must be stated by 
62 the General Court expressly and in plain language. 
63  (h) The General Court further determines that the same speed and the same nominal cost 
64 reach the Commonwealth itself as purchaser. When the state acquires an AI system outside the 
65 competitive evaluation, the public accountability, and the disclosure of conflicts of interest that 
66 the people require of all other public purchasing, the choice is again made for the residents and 
67 not by them. The purchasing of AI systems by the Commonwealth must be held to the standards 
68 the people have always required of the purchasing done in their name. A system demonstrated 
69 under the conditions of its maker's choosing has not been demonstrated under the conditions of 
70 the Commonwealth's use, and the difference between those two sets of conditions is borne by the 
71 resident. 
72  (i) The General Court further determines that the administration of the transparency 
73 rights, certifications, and community understanding established by this act must be entrusted to 
74 an entity captured neither by any single branch of government nor by industry, so that the trust 
75 the act is built to create is not undone by the body that administers it. 
76  SECTION 2. Purpose. 
77  The purpose of this Act is to: 
78  (a) establish that every resident of the Commonwealth has the right to know when an AI 
79 system is used in a consequential decision around their life, at the moment of interaction where 
80 the system meets the person and not merely at the moment the system was trained; 

5 of 58 81  (b) protect the authority of municipalities to require, if they choose, transparency around 
82 AI systems operating in their communities, an authority that shall not be diminished by this Act 
83 and shall not be taken away except as the General Court provides expressly and in plain 
84 language; 
85  (c) recognize that the impacts of physical AI infrastructure cross beyond the boundaries 
86 of the facility's site and into the community itself, and protect the authority of municipalities to 
87 require regular and transparent reporting of those impacts, drawn from readily available data and 
88 expressed in common, explainable terms and units; 
89  (d) ensure that residents know when the data generated by their interactions with an AI 
90 system is retained, used to train or improve the system, or shared; and that the value extracted 
91 from a community is made visible and transparent to the community itself, in accessible and 
92 common language; 
93  (e) ensure that when the Commonwealth purchases an AI system, the purchase is held to 
94 the same competitive evaluation, the same public accountability, and the same disclosure of 
95 conflicts of interest that the people require of all other public purchasing; and to provide that the 
96 Commonwealth, as purchaser, shall require of any vendor a plain and truthful accounting of 
97 whether the system can disclose its use, its outputs, and its inferences to the people it is used 
98 upon; that the Commonwealth shall have the right to audit the inferences of any such system 
99 across the linguistic, socioeconomic, and demographic subgroups of its residents; and that the 
100 Commonwealth shall choose the system that can be transparent and honest to the people it 
101 serves, so that what cannot be disclosed today for want of technological capability is disclosed 
102 tomorrow; 

6 of 58 103  (f) build a statewide understanding, grounded in the communities themselves, of how 
104 artificial intelligence is experienced by the residents it is used upon, across the cities and towns 
105 of the Commonwealth, recognizing that human understanding must be the cornerstone upon 
106 which any approach to AI is built; 
107  (g) establish the Massachusetts AI Trust Authority as an independent public entity, 
108 captured neither by any single branch of government nor by industry, to administer the voluntary 
109 AI Trust Certification, the Innovation Sandbox, the reciprocity frameworks, and the Community 
110 Advisory Board established by this Act, and to receive the accountings and conduct the audits 
111 provided for in this Act; 
112  (h) make the Commonwealth the place where trustworthy artificial intelligence is built 
113 and deployed, by rewarding transparency over invisibility, and by drawing responsible 
114 investment through incentive, collaboration, and reciprocity rather than mandate; 
115  (i) demonstrate that transparency at the moment the system meets the person is feasible to 
116 the measure of the technological capability these systems already possess, and that the duty to 
117 disclose shall grow as technological capability grows; achievable without the compelled 
118 disclosure of trade secrets, training data, or the proprietary internal architectures of any model; 
119 and that it protects the cognitive sovereignty of the person and, through it, the human condition 
120 itself. This Act offers that model to the governance of AI at the state and federal levels, to 
121 complement and to strengthen it; 
122  (j) extend transparency to transactions in which an AI system acts on behalf of a resident, 
123 so that the right to know does not diminish or fail as these systems evolve from tools, to agents, 
124 to collective swarms; and 

7 of 58 125  (k) ensure that no resident of the Commonwealth is left behind as artificial intelligence 
126 reshapes the institutions, services, opportunities, and personal growth of daily life. 
127  SECTION 3. The General Laws are hereby amended by inserting after chapter 93L the 
128 following chapter: 93M 
129  CHAPTER 93M. THE MASSACHUSETTS DIGITAL COMMONWEALTH 
130  Section 1. Definitions. 
131  As used in this chapter, the following words shall have the following meanings unless the 
132 context clearly requires otherwise: 
133  "Artificial intelligence system" or "AI system", any machine-based system that, for a 
134 given set of objectives, generates outputs such as predictions, recommendations, decisions, or 
135 content that can influence real or virtual environments, including but not limited to machine 
136 learning systems, large language models, automated decision-making systems, and AI agents as 
137 defined herein. 
138  "AI agent", an AI system configured to take autonomous actions on behalf of a resident, 
139 including but not limited to searching, evaluating, negotiating, selecting, transacting, or 
140 communicating, without requiring contemporaneous human confirmation for each action. An AI 
141 agent includes a system of multiple AI agents acting in coordination, in sequence, or in concert, 
142 whether alike or specialized, however composed and however distributed, and the obligations of 
143 this chapter shall apply to such a system as a whole. 
144  "Agent provider", the entity that makes an AI agent available to a resident and that the 
145 resident has authorized, directly or through use of the agent, to act on the resident's behalf. 

8 of 58 146  "Authority", the Massachusetts AI Trust Authority established under section 9. 
147  "Certified AI system", an AI system holding a valid AI Trust Certification issued by the 
148 Authority under this chapter. 
149  "Community Advisory Board" or "Board", the Community Advisory Board established 
150 under section 10. 
151  "Consequential decision", a decision, recommendation, or determination for which an AI 
152 system is a substantial factor and which has a material legal or similarly significant effect on the 
153 provision or denial to a resident of, or the cost or terms of: 
154  (1) employment or an employment opportunity; 
155  (2) housing; 
156  (3) credit or financial services; 
157  (4) insurance; 
158  (5) education enrollment or an educational opportunity; 
159  (6) healthcare services; 
160  (7) public benefits or essential government services; 
161  (8) legal services; or 
162  (9) engagement with the criminal justice system. 
163  A determination shall also constitute a consequential decision, regardless of whether it 
164 falls within the categories enumerated above, if the AI system engages in inference-time 

9 of 58 165 profiling of the resident in the course of generating its output; provided, however, that where 
166 such a determination does not have a material legal or similarly significant effect on the resident, 
167 the obligations arising under section 2 shall be limited to the matters described in section 2(a)(1), 
168 (2), (4), and (5), and may be satisfied by a clear and conspicuous disclosure presented within the 
169 interface through which the resident interacts with the AI system, applicable in standardized 
170 form to all interactions under the same deployment. 
171  The term "consequential decision" does not include determinations made by AI systems 
172 performing exclusively: (i) narrow procedural tasks; (ii) pattern detection or anomaly flagging 
173 where the output is reviewed by a human who has the authority and the practical opportunity to 
174 alter or override the outcome before it affects the resident; or (iii) only the following functions: 
175 anti-fraud detection not employing facial recognition, cybersecurity or anti-malware operations, 
176 spam filtering, spell-checking, grammar correction, calculation, routine data storage or retrieval, 
177 or language translation; provided, however, that any such system that engages in inference-time 
178 profiling of a resident shall remain subject to the transparency obligations of section 2 
179 notwithstanding this exclusion. 
180  The Authority may, by regulation, add categories of decisions to this definition based on 
181 findings reported by the Community Advisory Board under section 10(g)(4), provided that any 
182 such addition satisfies the material legal or similarly significant effect standard established by 
183 this section. The Authority may not, by regulation, remove any category enumerated in this 
184 section. 
185  "Covered facility", physical AI infrastructure with a design critical information 
186 technology load of not less than 5 megawatts, together with any colocated or contiguous physical 

10 of 58 187 AI infrastructure under common ownership or control the combined design critical information 
188 technology load of which is not less than 5 megawatts. 
189  "Delegated transaction", an action taken by an AI agent on behalf of a resident that 
190 commits the resident to a financial obligation, alters a contractual relationship, or affects access 
191 to a service, without contemporaneous human confirmation of that specific action. 
192  "Deployer", any person, entity, or government agency that deploys or uses an AI system 
193 within the commonwealth, or that deploys or uses an AI system, wherever operated, upon the 
194 residents of the commonwealth, whether as a developer, operator, or procuring entity. 
195  "Deployment objective", the objective or objectives established for an AI system in a 
196 given deployment, including any objective embodied in instructions, configurations, or 
197 parameters provided to the system at or before inference, whether established by the deployer, 
198 the vendor, or any other party. A deployment objective is stated as purpose and effect, in 
199 accessible and common language, and not as the verbatim content of any instruction, 
200 configuration, or parameter, and may be stated in a standardized form applicable to all 
201 interactions under the same deployment. The objective of a deployment directed at a resident is 
202 not a secret from that resident. 
203  "Determination", the output or conclusion an AI system produces at inference with 
204 respect to a resident, as distinct from the decision made concerning the resident. 
205  "Enterprise AI deployment", any procurement, contract, or agreement providing AI 
206 system access to more than 500 state employees or affecting services delivered to more than 
207 10,000 residents. 

11 of 58 208  "Facility impact report", a written report of the community impacts of a covered facility 
209 as provided in section 4, drawn from readily available data and expressed in common, 
210 explainable units. 
211  "Inference", the process by which a deployed AI system generates outputs, including 
212 predictions, recommendations, assessments, classifications, or content, in response to inputs, 
213 including inputs derived from or about a specific individual. Inference occurs at the point of 
214 impact: it is the moment at which a model's capabilities are applied to a real person or situation. 
215  "Inference audit", an examination, conducted by or at the direction of the Authority, of 
216 the outputs and inferences of an AI system as deployed, to identify disparities in the performance 
217 of those inferences across the linguistic, socioeconomic, and demographic subgroups of the 
218 residents of the commonwealth, and across the conditions under which the system is used, 
219 including variation in the phrasing, language, context, and channel of the interaction, and 
220 including the sustained interactions in which the system is used in practice. An audit conducted 
221 only under the conditions of the vendor's own evaluation is not an inference audit. 
222  "Inference-time profiling", the process by which an AI system, during the course of 
223 generating an output, assesses, classifies, or infers characteristics about an individual, including 
224 but not limited to emotional state, cognitive patterns, behavioral tendencies, demographic 
225 attributes, vulnerabilities, or susceptibility to influence, for the purpose of modifying the output 
226 delivered to that individual. 
227  "Massachusetts Municipal Association" or "MMA", the nonprofit membership 
228 association representing the cities and towns of the commonwealth, or its successor organization, 
229 acting in its capacity as a named consultation partner under this chapter. 

12 of 58 230  "Material financial relationship", any relationship in which an individual, or the 
231 individual's employer, parent company, or subsidiary, holds an ownership stake, investment 
232 interest, contractual partnership, or revenue-sharing arrangement with a vendor or prospective 
233 vendor, or has received compensation from such vendor within the preceding 36 months. 
234  "Material modification", with respect to a certified AI system, any change in its training 
235 data sources, its inference behavior on standardized benchmarks, its deployment scope, or its 
236 user population that the Authority by regulation determines to be material. The Authority shall 
237 establish the specific thresholds and criteria for material modification by rule and may update 
238 such criteria from time to time. 
239  "Physical AI infrastructure", a data center or other physical facility a principal function of 
240 which is to house computing, storage, or networking equipment used in the training of AI 
241 systems or in the operation of AI systems, including inference. 
242  "Readily available data", data that the owner or operator of a covered facility collects, 
243 generates, or receives in the ordinary course of designing, constructing, or operating the facility, 
244 or that the owner or operator reports to any federal or state agency, public utility, water or sewer 
245 authority, or independent system operator, together with data derivable from the foregoing 
246 without material additional instrumentation. 
247  "Substantial factor", with respect to the role of an AI system in a decision, 
248 recommendation, or determination, that the AI system both (i) assists in making the decision, 
249 recommendation, or determination, and (ii) is capable of altering the outcome. The mere 
250 involvement of an AI system in a peripheral, administrative, or ministerial function that is not 
251 capable of altering the outcome shall not constitute a substantial factor. 

13 of 58 252  "Transparency", the disclosure of sufficient information about an AI system's purpose, 
253 data inputs, logic, capabilities, limitations, and performance to enable meaningful awareness by 
254 affected residents and oversight by governing bodies. Disclosure that cannot be understood by 
255 the person it is owed to is not transparency. 
256  "Truthful accounting", a written disclosure, attested by an officer or principal of the 
257 vendor, stating in accessible and common language whether, and to what extent, an AI system 
258 can disclose its use, its outputs, and its inferences to the people it is used upon, including a 
259 statement of what the system cannot yet disclose and why. An accounting that omits a known 
260 limitation of the system is not truthful. 
261  "Trust Fund", the Massachusetts AI Trust Fund established under section 13. 
262  "Vendor", any person or entity that offers, sells, licenses, leases, or otherwise provides an 
263 AI system, or access to an AI system, to the commonwealth or to any deployer within the 
264 commonwealth, whether directly or through a reseller, integrator, or purchasing vehicle. 
265  Section 2. Right to Know. 
266  (a) Every resident of the commonwealth has the right to know when an AI system is used 
267 to make, inform, or materially influence a determination in a consequential decision affecting 
268 one's life. The right begins at inference. It does not begin at training, and no claim about training 
269 data, design, or authorship diminishes it. The right belongs to the resident; the duty to disclose 
270 and provide transparent understanding of the outcome extends to any affected individual. The 
271 right to know includes: 
272  (1) that an AI system was used in the decision; 

14 of 58 273  (2) the purpose and function of the system, and the deployment objective, stated in 
274 commonly understandable language; 
275  (3) the categories of data concerning the resident that the system processed at the time of 
276 inference; 
277  (4) whether the system assessed, profiled, or inferred characteristics of the individual in 
278 the course of generating its output; 
279  (5) whether information provided by the resident, or otherwise processed at inference, is 
280 retained, used to train or improve the AI system, or is shared with any third party; 
281  (6) how long information transmitted during inference is stored; 
282  (7) how often the system's performance is reviewed, and whether those reviews catch 
283 changes in how the system behaves after its deployment; 
284  (8) how the system behaves under different conditions, including the conditions under 
285 which its performance degrades; 
286  (9) how to contest or challenge the decision, with clear and understandable means to 
287 reach a human being with sufficient authority to review and audit the decision; and 
288  (10) the identity of the deployer, and whether any third party was used by the deployer in 
289 reaching the determination. 
290  (b) This section grants no authority to restrict, delay, condition, or prohibit the 
291 development, deployment, or use of any AI system, and none shall be inferred from it. It 
292 establishes a right to visibility, not a right to veto. 

15 of 58 293  (c) Disclosure must follow the point of inference. Where inference occurs as part of a 
294 human-AI system interaction, the deployer shall provide disclosure on how the outcome was 
295 determined, with any relevant context, in common language, at or before the moment of the 
296 determination. Where inference occurs without the person present, the disclosure must travel to 
297 them; the deployer shall provide direct notice to the affected individual at or before the moment 
298 of the decision, and where notice before is impracticable, within a reasonable time, and never 
299 more than 30 days after. A posting on a website or in terms of service may support the required 
300 disclosure but does not satisfy it. The disclosure must be explainable, and a disclosure the 
301 average individual cannot understand does not satisfy this requirement. A disclosure the person 
302 had no reasonable occasion to encounter was never made. 
303  (d) This section requires no disclosure of proprietary algorithms or architectures, training 
304 data, model weights, or trade secrets, or any other confidential intellectual property. 
305 Documentation or auditing of training data is not required. Transparency under this section is 
306 defined as disclosure to inform and provide explainable context, not disclosure with sufficient 
307 information to replicate or repeat. The obligations of this section attach solely to the application 
308 of the AI system, at inference, to the affected individual. 
309  (e) Delegated transactions. 
310  (1) When an AI agent undertakes a delegated transaction on behalf of a resident, the 
311 agent provider shall maintain a reviewable record of the transaction and shall make such record 
312 available to the resident in plain language within a reasonable time following the transaction. 
313 The record shall include: 
314  (A) a description of the action taken; 

16 of 58 315  (B) the alternatives considered by the agent, if any, and the basis for selecting the action 
316 taken; 
317  (C) any inference-time profiling performed on the resident or the resident's agent in the 
318 course of the transaction, to the extent known by the agent provider; 
319  (D) any financial or contractual consequences of the action for the resident; and 
320  (E) the identity of the agent provider and any third-party AI systems or platforms 
321 involved in the transaction, to the extent known by the agent provider. 
322  (2) The agent provider bears primary responsibility for the disclosure obligations of this 
323 subsection for the full chain of AI systems and third parties involved in executing a delegated 
324 transaction on behalf of a resident, regardless of the number or identity of intermediary systems. 
325  (3) Agent providers shall disclose to residents, prior to the resident's authorization of the 
326 agent, any preferred-vendor relationships, affiliate arrangements, or revenue-sharing incentives 
327 that may influence the agent's decisions. 
328  (4) The Authority may establish by regulation thresholds below which the record 
329 requirement of paragraph (1) does not apply. Such thresholds shall be designed to exclude 
330 routine, low-value, de minimis transactions while preserving the record requirement for 
331 transactions of material consequence. 
332  (f) Enforcement. 
333  (1) A knowing and willful failure to provide the disclosures required by this section in 
334 connection with a consequential decision or delegated transaction shall constitute an unfair or 
335 deceptive act or practice under chapter 93A of the General Laws. A resident who demonstrates 

17 of 58 336 that a consequential decision was made affecting them, or that a delegated transaction was 
337 undertaken on their behalf, without the disclosures required by this section may seek damages 
338 under the procedures and remedies established by said chapter 93A. The deprivation of the 
339 disclosures required by this section, including the loss of the opportunity to contest the 
340 determination and to reach human review as provided in subsection (a)(9), constitutes a distinct 
341 injury to the resident, separate from the violation itself. 
342  (2) A resident may seek injunctive or declaratory relief to compel the disclosures required 
343 by this section upon a showing that a deployer or agent provider has materially failed to provide 
344 such disclosures and that the resident is or will imminently be subject to a consequential decision 
345 or delegated transaction by the AI system. A court may grant preliminary injunctive relief under 
346 this paragraph upon a finding that the resident is likely to succeed on the merits and that the 
347 balance of equities favors disclosure. The court shall not require a showing of knowing and 
348 willful conduct as a condition of injunctive or declaratory relief under this paragraph. Relief 
349 under this paragraph shall be limited to requiring compliance with the disclosure obligations of 
350 this section and shall not extend to restricting the operation of any AI system. 
351  (3) The attorney general may bring an action for injunctive relief, declaratory relief, or 
352 civil penalties under chapter 93A, section 4, to enforce the disclosure requirements of this section 
353 against any deployer or agent provider. The attorney general may, prior to commencing an 
354 action, provide the deployer or agent provider with written notice of the alleged violation and a 
355 reasonable opportunity to achieve compliance with the disclosure requirements of this section. In 
356 determining whether to bring an action or to provide an opportunity to achieve compliance, the 
357 attorney general may consider the scope and frequency of non-compliance, the number of 
358 residents affected, and whether the deployer or agent provider has taken good-faith steps to 

18 of 58 359 comply or has been responsive to prior inquiries. Nothing in this paragraph limits the authority of 
360 the attorney general under chapter 93A or any other provision of law. 
361  (4) Nothing in this subsection creates a new cause of action for damages beyond those 
362 available under chapter 93A; it connects the transparency right established by this section to the 
363 existing enforcement framework of the commonwealth while ensuring that injunctive relief 
364 remains practically available to affected residents and that the attorney general may address 
365 systemic non-compliance. 
366  (5) With respect to a deployer that is a government agency not engaged in trade or 
367 commerce within the meaning of chapter 93A, the remedies available under this subsection shall 
368 be those provided in paragraph (2), together with any other remedy available under law; and the 
369 affected resident shall in addition have the right to review of the determination through the 
370 means required by subsection (a)(9). 
371  (g) Vendor support obligation. A vendor that provides an AI system used by a deployer in 
372 consequential decisions shall supply to the deployer, in standardized form, accurate information 
373 within the vendor's knowledge and control reasonably necessary for the deployer to satisfy the 
374 disclosure obligations of this section, and shall update such information upon any material 
375 modification to the system. A deployer's disclosure made in good-faith and reasonable reliance 
376 on information supplied under this subsection satisfies the deployer's obligation with respect to 
377 the matters so supplied, and responsibility for any inaccuracy in such information rests with the 
378 vendor. Nothing in this subsection requires the disclosure by a vendor of the materials described 
379 in subsection (d). 
380  Section 3. Municipal Authority. 

19 of 58 381  (a) Municipalities of the commonwealth shall possess inherent authority, consistent with 
382 the Home Rule Amendment and the general laws, to require transparency regarding AI systems 
383 deployed or operating within their jurisdictions that affect their residents. This authority includes 
384 the authority to: 
385  (1) require deployers to disclose, upon request, the purpose, general function, and 
386 categories of data inputs for AI systems used in consequential decisions affecting municipal 
387 residents; 
388  (2) require that residents affected by consequential AI decisions within the municipality 
389 have access to the information described in section 2; and 
390  (3) participate in voluntary municipal cooperation frameworks for shared transparency 
391 infrastructure. 
392  (b) Municipal authority under this section extends to transparency and visibility. Nothing 
393 in this section shall be construed to grant municipalities authority to prohibit, restrict, condition, 
394 license, or impose operational requirements upon the development, training, deployment, or use 
395 of AI systems. Municipalities may require that AI be visible. Municipalities may not require that 
396 AI be absent. 
397  (c) No state agency, executive order, regulation, or administrative action shall restrict, 
398 diminish, or preempt the authority of municipalities to require transparency regarding AI systems 
399 within their jurisdictions as affirmed by this section. 
400  (d) Rule of construction. No provision of the general laws, whether enacted before or 
401 after the effective date of this section, shall be construed to restrict, diminish, or preempt the 

20 of 58 402 authority of municipalities to require transparency regarding AI systems within their jurisdictions 
403 as affirmed by this section, unless such provision specifically and explicitly states that it is 
404 intended to restrict, diminish, or preempt municipal AI transparency authority established under 
405 this chapter. 
406  (e) Any ambiguity in a provision of the general laws regarding its effect on municipal AI 
407 transparency authority shall be resolved in favor of preserving such authority. 
408  (f) A municipality may seek injunctive relief to enforce the transparency requirements of 
409 this section through appropriate proceedings in the superior court. 
410  (g) Nothing in this section shall be construed to limit the authority of the General Court to 
411 establish minimum standards for AI transparency that municipalities may meet or exceed. 
412  (h) Municipal self-governance. Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to limit the 
413 authority of a municipality to decide whether, and on what terms, it procures, adopts, declines, 
414 discontinues, or uses AI systems in its own operations and in the services it delivers, and no state 
415 agency, executive order, regulation, or administrative action shall compel such adoption or 
416 interfere with such decisions. The limitations of subsection (b) concern the regulation by a 
417 municipality of persons other than the municipality itself and do not diminish the authority 
418 affirmed by this subsection. 
419  Section 4. Physical AI Infrastructure Transparency. 
420  (a) The impacts of a covered facility cross beyond the boundaries of the facility's site and 
421 into the community itself. A municipality in which a covered facility is located, in whole or in 
422 part, may require the owner or operator of the covered facility to prepare and provide facility 

21 of 58 423 impact reports as set forth in this section. A municipality that borders a municipality in which a 
424 covered facility is located, and that shares with the covered facility a water source, sewer system, 
425 or electric distribution infrastructure, may require that the facility impact reports prepared under 
426 this section be provided to it on the same terms. 
427  (b) A facility impact report shall state, for the reporting period, drawn from readily 
428 available data and expressed in common, explainable units:

437  (4) whether information generated by the interactions of residents of the commonwealth 
438 with AI systems operated from the facility is retained at the facility, used to train or improve AI 
439 systems, or shared, stated in accessible and common language, to the extent known by the owner

22 of 58 443  (c) A municipality may require facility impact reports not more frequently than quarterly.

449 other confidential intellectual property.

23 of 58 464  (g) A municipality may seek injunctive relief to enforce the requirements of this section

474  Section 5. State Procurement of AI Systems. 
475  (a) This section shall apply to any enterprise AI deployment by the commonwealth or any 
476 state agency. 
477  (b) No enterprise AI deployment shall proceed without a documented competitive 
478 evaluation of not fewer than 3 qualified vendors, conducted through a publicly accessible 
479 procurement process. The procurement process shall include: 
480  (1) a public solicitation published on COMMBUYS or its successor system with not 
481 fewer than 45 days for vendor response; 
482  (2) published evaluation criteria, including technical capability, security certification, 
483 data protection, cost, and interoperability; 

24 of 58 484  (3) a written evaluation report comparing all responsive vendors against each published 
485 criterion; 
486  (4) publication of the evaluation report, redacted only as required by law to protect 
487 genuine trade secrets, within 30 days of contract award; and 
488  (5) submission by each responsive vendor of a truthful accounting of the AI system 
489 offered, including a statement of the conditions under which the vendor has evaluated the system 
490 and the conditions under which it has not. No vendor shall be deemed responsive without one. 
491 The truthful accounting of the vendor awarded the contract shall be published with the evaluation 
492 report under paragraph (4), and the capability of each offered system to disclose its use, its 
493 outputs, and its inferences to the people it is used upon shall be a published evaluation criterion 
494 under paragraph (2). 
495  (c) Any individual serving on an advisory body, task force, evaluation committee, or 
496 other entity that recommends, evaluates, or influences the selection of an AI vendor for state 
497 procurement shall file a public disclosure identifying: 
498  (1) any material financial relationship between the individual, or the individual's 
499 employer, and any prospective vendor; 
500  (2) any campaign contributions made by the individual to any elected official with 
501 authority over the procurement within the preceding 48 months; and 
502  (3) any board membership, advisory role, or consulting arrangement with any prospective 
503 vendor or the vendor's investors, partners, or parent company. 

25 of 58 504  (d) Disclosures required under subsection (c) shall be filed with the office of the inspector 
505 general and made publicly available on the commonwealth's procurement transparency portal 
506 within 14 days of the individual's appointment or, if later, within 14 days of the commencement 
507 of any procurement process. 
508  (e) An individual with a material financial relationship to a prospective vendor shall 
509 recuse themselves from any evaluation, recommendation, or vote pertaining to that vendor. 
510 Failure to disclose or recuse shall constitute a violation of chapter 268A and shall be referred to 
511 the state ethics commission. 
512  (f) No enterprise AI deployment shall be implemented without a consultation period of 
513 not fewer than 60 days during which affected public employees and their designated 
514 representatives may review the proposed deployment, submit comments, and receive written 
515 responses to substantive concerns. Where a collective bargaining agreement covers affected 
516 employees, the deployment shall be subject to the applicable provisions of such agreement 
517 regarding the introduction of new technology. 
518  (g) The procuring agency shall publish a workforce impact assessment identifying 
519 anticipated effects on job functions, required training, data access, and working conditions. 
520  (h) No enterprise AI deployment contract shall exceed a term of 3 years without renewal. 
521 Renewal shall require a new competitive evaluation under the standards of this section. 
522  (i) Every enterprise AI deployment shall include a quarterly performance review, the 
523 results of which shall be published and made available to the General Court, affected workers, 
524 and the public. Quarterly reviews shall address system performance, accuracy, observed drift as 
525 measured against the conditions of the evaluation conducted under subsection (b), fine-tuning or 

26 of 58 526 other material modifications since the prior review, and any issues identified by affected workers 
527 or residents. 
528  (j) The General Court shall receive notice of any proposed enterprise AI deployment not 
529 fewer than 60 days prior to contract execution, including the proposed vendor, contract value, 
530 scope of deployment, and a summary of the competitive evaluation. 
531  (k) The state auditor shall have authority to audit any enterprise AI deployment for 
532 compliance with the procurement standards established by this section. The inspector general 
533 shall have authority to investigate complaints regarding conflicts of interest, procurement 
534 irregularities, or violations of this section. 
535  (l) Every enterprise AI deployment contract shall provide that the Authority may conduct, 
536 or direct the conduct of, inference audits of the deployed AI system. The results of each 
537 inference audit shall be published with the quarterly performance review under subsection (i), 
538 redacted only as required by law, and shall be provided to the Community Advisory Board. 
539  (m) This section shall apply to enterprise AI deployment contracts entered into or 
540 renewed on or after the effective date of this act. Nothing in this section shall be construed to 
541 invalidate or require renegotiation of contracts entered into prior to said effective date. 
542  (n) Exceptions. The requirements of subsections (b), (f), and (j) may be modified or 
543 waived only upon written findings by the secretary of administration and finance, published 
544 within 14 days and provided to the inspector general and the General Court, that: (1) fewer than 
545 3 qualified vendors exist for the system sought, in which case the competitive evaluation shall 
546 include all qualified vendors; (2) an emergency affecting cybersecurity, public health, or public 
547 safety requires deployment before the periods prescribed can run, in which case any resulting 

27 of 58 548 contract shall not exceed 12 months and any renewal shall comply with this section in full; (3) 
549 the system is required by federal law or as a condition of federal funds; or (4) the deployment is 
550 a pilot of not more than 6 months' duration and $250,000 in value, not renewable except through 
551 full compliance with this section. Every waiver under this subsection shall be time-limited, shall 
552 be subject to audit under subsection (k), and shall not modify or waive the disclosure and 
553 publication requirements of this section. 
554  Section 6. Community AI Understanding Program. 
555  (a) The General Court finds that Massachusetts shall lead in the human-centered

557 without a grounded understanding of how AI is encountered, understood, and experienced by 
558 residents across the commonwealth's diverse communities. 
559  (b) There is hereby established the Community AI Understanding Program, administered 
560 by the Authority in partnership with the Massachusetts Municipal Association as provided under 
561 section 11, through which municipalities may conduct annual assessments of how artificial

563 shall be voluntary.

565 municipality deems appropriate, including but not limited to a town meeting agenda item, a 
566 public forum, a resident survey, a report prepared by municipal staff or a designated appointee,

569 government channels. The assessment should address: 

28 of 58 570  (1) how AI systems are currently encountered and experienced by residents of the

574  (3) any consequential decisions affecting residents that are known to involve AI systems; 
575  (4) the state of community understanding and readiness with respect to artificial 
576 intelligence; and 
577  (5) the extent to which data generated by the interactions of residents with AI systems is 
578 known to be retained, used to train or improve such systems, or shared, and any facility impact 
579 reports provided under section 4 to the municipality during the assessment period.

581 Advisory Board established in section 10 for inclusion in the statewide synthesis. 
582  (e) Nothing in this section creates any regulatory authority, permitting requirement, 
583 review process, or mechanism by which a municipality may delay, condition, or restrict the

597  Section 7. AI Trust Certification, Innovation Sandbox, and Reciprocity. 
598  (a) The General Court finds that transparency and innovation are mutually reinforcing. 
599 Jurisdictions that establish clear, predictable standards for AI transparency create conditions of 
600 trust that attract responsible investment, expand market access, and accelerate the development 
601 and deployment of trustworthy AI systems. The purpose of this section is to position the 
602 Commonwealth of Massachusetts as the premier jurisdiction for the development and 
603 deployment of transparent, trustworthy AI. The General Court intends that the transparency 
604 standards established by this chapter serve as a model for AI governance at the state and federal 
605 level and shall continue in full force unless expressly preempted by federal law. 
606  (b) AI Trust Certification. 
607  (1) There is hereby established the Massachusetts AI Trust Certification, a voluntary 
608 program administered by the Authority under which AI developers and deployers may earn a 
609 state-backed certification demonstrating compliance with transparency and accountability 
610 standards. 

30 of 58 611  (2) The AI Trust Certification program shall: 
612  (A) establish tiered certification levels reflecting the degree and scope of transparency 
613 and accountability demonstrated by the applicant, including at minimum a foundational tier 
614 based on self-attestation to recognized standards, an intermediate tier based on third-party 
615 verification, and an advanced tier based on internationally recognized management-system 
616 certification; 
617  (B) where appropriate, reference and incorporate existing recognized frameworks, 
618 including but not limited to the National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk 
619 Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, and comparable international standards, to promote 
620 interoperability; 
621  (C) publish certification criteria, application procedures, and evaluation standards in 
622 advance and make them freely available to prospective applicants; 
623  (D) conduct evaluations through a transparent review process, with results published 
624 within 60 days of application; 
625  (E) issue certifications valid for a period of 2 years, renewable through demonstrated 
626 continued compliance; 
627  (F) specify the scope of each certification, including the specific AI systems and 
628 deployment contexts covered, and require certified entities to use the certification mark only in 
629 connection with that specified scope; and 

31 of 58 630  (G) maintain a public registry of all certified entities, including the tier of certification, 
631 the scope of certification, the date of issuance and expiration, and the status of any open formal 
632 inquiries or revocation proceedings under section 10. 
633  (3) A certified entity shall notify the Authority within 30 days of any material 
634 modification to a certified AI system. The Authority shall by regulation establish the criteria for 
635 material modification, procedures for review, and timelines for re-evaluation, which shall be 
636 proportionate to the risk and scope of the modification. 
637  (c) Entities holding a current Massachusetts AI Trust Certification shall be eligible for: 
638  (1) preferred consideration in state and municipal AI procurement processes, provided 
639 such preference does not override the competitive evaluation requirements of section 5 of this 
640 chapter; 
641  (2) eligibility for innovation grants funded through appropriations designated for the 
642 purposes of this section or through the Trust Fund; 
643  (3) expedited transparency compliance within participating municipalities, under which a 
644 current certification may be presented as presumptive evidence of compliance with municipal 
645 transparency requirements, to the extent such requirements fall within the scope of the 
646 certification; and 
647  (4) use of the Massachusetts AI Trust Certification mark in commercial materials, subject 
648 to terms established by the Authority, provided that the mark may be used only in connection 
649 with the specific AI systems and deployment contexts within the scope of the certification, and 
650 any use of the mark shall disclose the tier and scope. 

32 of 58 651  (d) Innovation Sandbox. 
652  (1) There is hereby established an Innovation Sandbox through which entities may deploy 
653 experimental AI systems in participating municipalities under enhanced transparency conditions. 
654 The Innovation Sandbox shall: 
655  (A) permit accelerated deployment timelines for AI systems that are not yet eligible for 
656 full AI Trust Certification, provided the deploying entity agrees to enhanced transparency and 
657 community feedback mechanisms exceeding the standard requirements of this chapter; 
658  (B) limit experimental deployments to a period of not more than 12 months, renewable 
659 once upon demonstration of satisfactory performance and community benefit, and subject to 
660 review by the Community Advisory Board; 
661  (C) require the informed consent of the participating municipality's governing body; 
662  (D) require the deploying entity to publish quarterly public reports detailing system 
663 performance, identified issues, community feedback received, and corrective actions taken; 
664  (E) provide a structured pathway from sandbox participation to full AI Trust 
665 Certification; and 
666  (F) require public disclosure of the reason for any exit from the sandbox without 
667 successful transition to full certification. 
668  (2) The Authority shall by regulation establish application procedures, participation 
669 criteria, data-usage limits, required consumer disclosures, reporting requirements, and fees for 
670 the Innovation Sandbox. Such regulations shall include substantive review of sandbox 

33 of 58 671 applications by the Community Advisory Board and a requirement that the Authority respond in 
672 writing to Board recommendations regarding sandbox admissions and exits. 
673  (e) Reciprocity. 
674  (1) The Authority shall actively seek reciprocity agreements with other states, regional 
675 compacts, federal agencies, and international jurisdictions that maintain AI transparency 
676 frameworks compatible with the standards established under this chapter. Reciprocity 
677 agreements may provide for: 
678  (A) mutual recognition of AI trust certifications or equivalent credentials; 
679  (B) shared technical standards for transparency and interoperability; 
680  (C) coordinated enforcement of AI transparency requirements; and 
681  (D) coordinated approaches to emerging AI transparency challenges. 
682  (2) Reciprocity agreements shall be approved by the Authority's governing board 
683 following equivalency analysis and consultation with the Community Advisory Board. No 
684 mutual recognition shall be extended to a partner jurisdiction unless the Authority finds that the 
685 partner jurisdiction's standards meet the floor established by this chapter. 
686  (3) An entity that has been denied Massachusetts certification on substantive grounds 
687 shall be ineligible for reciprocity-based operation in the commonwealth for a period to be 
688 established by the Authority by regulation. 
689  (f) Annual Innovation and Trust Report. The Authority shall publish an annual 
690 Innovation and Trust Report assessing the effectiveness of the AI Trust Certification, Innovation 

34 of 58 691 Sandbox, and reciprocity programs, including: the number of certifications issued, renewed, 
692 suspended, and revoked; the scope and tier distribution of certifications; the economic impact of 
693 certified entities within the commonwealth; the outcomes of sandbox deployments; the status of 
694 reciprocity agreements; the status of AI transparency legislation in other states and at the federal 
695 level; and recommendations for maintaining the commonwealth's leadership position. The report 
696 shall be submitted to the General Court, the Community Advisory Board, and the Massachusetts 
697 Municipal Association, and shall be made publicly available.

1138  Section 14. Limitations. 
1139  (a) Except for the disclosure, recordkeeping, reporting, auditing, procurement, 
1140 certification, and facility impact reporting obligations expressly established by this chapter, 
1141 nothing in this chapter shall be construed to: 
1142  (1) prohibit, restrict, condition, license, or impose operational requirements upon the 
1143 development, training, deployment, or use of any artificial intelligence system; provided, 
1144 however, that the facility impact reporting authorized by section 4 concerns the physical 
1145 operation of covered facilities and does not constitute an operational requirement upon any AI 
1146 system within the meaning of this paragraph; 
1147  (2) create any regulatory authority, permitting requirement, or approval process for AI 
1148 systems at the state or municipal level; 
1149  (3) require the disclosure of proprietary algorithms, trade secrets, model weights, training 
1150 data, or other confidential intellectual property; the disclosure obligations of section 2 attach 

56 of 58 1151 solely to the application of the AI system, at inference, to the affected individual, and shall not be 
1152 construed to require any examination of or inquiry into training data, training methodology, or 
1153 model development processes; 
1154  (4) require the documentation, inventory, or auditing of training data used to develop 
1155 foundation models or large language models, the General Court having found that, as of the 
1156 effective date of this Act, such requirements are technically infeasible for these systems using 
1157 currently available methods and do not address the inference-time risks that are the subject of 
1158 this chapter; provided that nothing in this subsection limits the authority of existing law to 
1159 require training data documentation for narrow AI systems where such documentation is 
1160 feasible; and provided further that this subsection shall not be construed to limit the authority of 
1161 the General Court to revisit training data documentation requirements as methods and technical 
1162 standards evolve; 
1163  (5) impose liability based solely on the composition or provenance of training data for 
1164 foundation models or large language models, the General Court having determined that the 
1165 relevant transparency obligation for these systems attaches at the point of impact on individuals, 
1166 not at the point of model development; or 
1167  (6) limit the right of any person or entity to develop, train, deploy, or use computational 
1168 resources for lawful purposes; the visibility provided in section 4 is not a limitation of that right. 
1169  (b) This chapter establishes transparency at the point of impact. It requires that when AI 
1170 systems make or substantially inform consequential decisions about Massachusetts residents, 
1171 those residents can see it. It extends the same principle to delegated transactions in which AI 
1172 systems act autonomously on behalf of residents, and to the community impacts of the physical 

57 of 58 1173 infrastructure from which these systems operate. It does not dictate how AI systems are built, 
1174 what data they are trained on, or how they operate internally. The Commonwealth of 
1175 Massachusetts welcomes the development and deployment of artificial intelligence and seeks to 
1176 create conditions of trust and visibility in which innovation thrives. 
1177  (c) Nothing in the general laws shall be construed to diminish municipal AI transparency 
1178 authority as affirmed by sections 3 and 4 of this chapter except by express legislative statement 
1179 specifically and explicitly identifying its intent to restrict, diminish, or preempt such authority. 
1180  (d) Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to limit any right, remedy, or protection 
1181 available to any resident, worker, or municipality under any other provision of the general laws, 
1182 federal law, or municipal ordinance.
