137 Section 2. Electronic monitoring tools.
7 of 32

138 (a) It shall be unlawful for an employer to use an electronic monitoring tool to collect
139 employee information unless:
140 (i) the electronic monitoring tool is primarily used to accomplish any of the following
141 purposes:
142 (A) allowing a worker to accomplish or facilitating the accomplishment of an essential
143 job function;
144 (B) ensuring the quality of goods and services;
145 (C) conducting periodic assessment of worker performance;
146 (D) ensuring or facilitating compliance with employment, labor, or other relevant laws;
147 (E) protecting the health, safety, or security of workers, or the security of the employer's
148 facilities or computer networks; or
149 (F) administering wages and benefits.
150 The department of labor standards may establish additional exceptions under clause (i)
151 through notice and comment rulemaking in compliance with chapter 30A.
152 Nothing in this Act shall diminish an Employer’s obligation under Massachusetts or
153 Federal Law to provide advance notice and to engage in good faith negotiations with a labor
154 organization representing any portion of its workforce before implementation.
155 (ii) the specific type and activated capabilities of an electronic monitoring tool must be
156 narrowly tailored to accomplish the employer’s intended, legitimate purpose specified under (i).
8 of 32

157 (iii) the electronic monitoring tool may only be used to accomplish the employer’s
158 intended, legitimate purpose specified in (i), and must be customized and implemented in a
159 manner ensuring that the execution of its duties undertaken in the manner least invasive to
160 employees of the employer while accomplishing the employer’s legitimate purposes as defined
161 by (i);
162 (iv) the specific form of electronic monitoring is limited to the smallest number of
163 workers, collects the least amount of data and is collected no more frequently than is necessary
164 to accomplish the purpose, and the data collected is deleted once the purpose has been achieved.
165 (v) the employer must ensure that any employee data that is collected utilizing an
166 electronic monitoring tool that is not necessary to accomplish the employer’s intended,
167 legitimate purpose is not disclosed to the employer and is promptly disposed of by the vendor;
168 (vi) the employer must ensure that employee data is not collected when the employee is
169 off-duty; and
170 (vii) the employer must ensure that any employee data collected utilizing an electronic
171 monitoring tool that is necessary to accomplish the employer’s intended, legitimate purpose, is
172 stored consistent with the commonwealth’s data- and cyber- privacy laws, promptly disposed of
173 as soon as the data is no longer needed, and is not utilized by the employer, the vendor or any
174 other third party for any reason except as provided in section 2(c) and section 3(c) of this
175 chapter.
176 (b) Any employer that uses an electronic monitoring tool shall give prior written notice
177 and must obtain written consent from all candidates and employees subject to electronic
178 monitoring and must also post said notice in a conspicuous place which is readily available for

179 viewing by candidates and employees, pursuant to sections 19B, 52C, and 190(i) of chapter 149

182 specified in subparagraph (i) of paragraph (a) of this subdivision;
183 (ii) a description of the specific employee data to be collected, stored, secured, and

330 Section 3. Automated decision tools used for employment-related decisions.
331 a) It shall be unlawful for an employer to use an automated employment decision tool for
332 an employment decision, alone or in conjunction with electronic monitoring, unless such tool has
333 been the subject of an impact assessment. Impact assessments must:
334 (i) be conducted no more than one year prior to the use of such tool, or where the tool
335 was in use by the employer before the effective date of this article, within six months of the
336 effective date of this article;
337 (ii) be conducted by an independent and impartial party with no financial or legal
338 conflicts of interest;
339 (iii) identify and describe the attributes and modeling techniques that the tool uses to
340 produce outputs;
341 (iv) evaluate whether those attributes and techniques are a scientifically valid means of
342 evaluating an employee or candidate's performance or ability to perform the essential functions
17 of 32

343 of a role, and whether those attributes may function as a proxy for belonging to a protected class
344 under chapter 151B or any other applicable law;
345 (v) consider, identify, and describe any disparities in the data used to train or develop the
346 tool and describe how those disparities may result in a disparate impact on persons based on their
347 race, color, religious creed, national origin, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, genetic
348 information, pregnancy or a condition related to said pregnancy including, but not limited to,
349 lactation or the need to express breast milk for a nursing child, ancestry or status as a veteran,
350 and what actions may be taken by the employer or vendor of the tool to reduce or remedy any
351 disparate impact;
352 (vi) consider, identify, and describe any outputs produced by the tool that may result in a
353 disparate impact on persons based on their race, color, religious creed, national origin, sex,
354 gender identity, sexual orientation, genetic information, pregnancy or a condition related to said
355 pregnancy including, but not limited to, lactation or the need to express breast milk for a nursing
356 child, ancestry or status as a veteran, and what actions may be taken by the employer or vendor
357 of the tool to reduce or remedy that disparate impact;
358 (vii) evaluate whether the use of the tool may limit accessibility for persons with
359 disabilities, or for persons with any specific disability, and what actions may be taken by the
360 employer or vendor of the tool to reduce or remedy the concern;
361 (viii) consider and describe potential sources of adverse impact against individuals or
362 groups based on race, color, religious creed, national origin, sex, gender identity, sexual
363 orientation, genetic information, pregnancy or a condition related to said pregnancy including,
18 of 32

364 but not limited to, lactation or the need to express breast milk for a nursing child, ancestry or
365 status as a veteran that may arise after the tool is deployed;
366 (ix) identify and describe any other assessment of risks of discrimination or a disparate
367 impact of the tool on individuals or groups based on race, color, religious creed, national origin,
368 sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, genetic information, pregnancy or a condition related to
369 said pregnancy including, but not limited to, lactation or the need to express breast milk for a
370 nursing child, ancestry or status as a veteran that arise over the course of the impact assessment,

372 (x) for any finding of a disparate impact or limit on accessibility, evaluate whether the
373 data set, attribute, or feature of the tool at issue is the least discriminatory method of assessing a

375 (xi) consider and describe any other ways in which the tool could result in a violation of
376 applicable law and, for any finding that a violation of law may occur, any necessary or
377 appropriate steps to prevent such violation of law;
378 (xii) consider and describe whether use of the tool may negatively impact employees’
379 privacy and job quality, including wages, hours, and working conditions; and
380 (xiii) be submitted in its entirety or an accessible summary form to the department for
381 inclusion in a public registry of such impact assessments within sixty days of completion and

383 (b) An employer shall conduct or commission subsequent impact assessments each year
384 that the tool is in use to assist or replace employment decisions. Subsequent impact assessments

385 shall comply with the requirements of paragraph (a) of this section, and shall assess and describe

400 conducting an impact assessment.
401 (d) If an initial or subsequent impact assessment requires the collection of employee data
402 to assess a tool's disparate impact on employees, such data shall be collected, processed, stored,

407 unless strictly necessary for the completion of the impact assessment.

473 Section 5. Restricted uses of automated decision tools.
474 (a) Notwithstanding the provisions of subdivision one of this section, an employer shall
475 not, alone or in conjunction with an electronic monitoring tool, use an automated decision tool:
476 (i) in such a manner that results in a violation of labor, employment, or civil rights law or
477 any other law of the commonwealth;
478 (ii) in a manner that harms or is likely to harm the health or safety of employees,
479 including by setting productivity quotas in a manner that is likely to cause physical or mental
480 illness or injury;
481 (iii) to make predictions about an employee or candidate for employment's behavior,
482 beliefs, intentions, personality, emotional state, or other characteristic or behavior;
483 (iv) to predict, interfere with, restrain, or coerce employees engaging in activity protected
484 under labor and employment law;
485 (v) to subtract from an employee's wages time spent exercising their legal rights;
24 of 32

486 (vi) in a manner that deviates from the specification of the automated employment
487 decision tool as implemented after the incorporation of any alterations made pursuant to the
488 impact assessment required by subdivision one of this section; or
489 (vii) that involves facial recognition, gait, or emotion recognition technologies.
490 (b) An employer shall not rely primarily on output from an automated decision tool when
491 making hiring, promotion, termination, disciplinary, or compensation decisions. For an employer
492 to satisfy the requirements of this paragraph:
493 (i) An employer must establish meaningful human oversight of such decisions based in
494 whole or in part on the output of automated employment decision tools.
495 In determining whether an internal reviewer employs the requisite knowledge and skill to
496 provide meaningful human oversight, relevant factors include the relative complexity and
497 specialized nature of the automated decision tool, the reviewer’s general experience, the
498 reviewer’s training and experience in the field, the preparation and study the reviewer is able to
499 give the matter and whether it is feasible to refer the matter to, or associate or consult with, an
500 expert with established competence in the field automated decision tools.
501 (ii) A human decision-maker must actually review any output of an automated
502 employment decision tool and exercise independent judgment in making each such decision;
503 (iii) The human decision-maker must consider information other than automated
504 employment decision tool outputs when making each such decision, such as but not limited to
505 supervisory or managerial evaluations, personnel files, employee work products, or peer reviews;
506 and

507 (iv) An employer shall consider information other than automated employment decision
508 tool outputs when making hiring, promotion, termination, disciplinary, or compensation
509 decisions, such as supervisory or managerial evaluations, personnel files, employee work
510 products, or peer reviews.
511 (c) An employer shall not require employees or candidates to consent to the use of an
512 automated employment decision tool in an employment decision in order to be considered for an
513 employment decision, nor shall an employer discipline or disadvantage an employee or candidate
514 for employment as a result of their request for accommodation.

518 Section 6. Anti-retaliation provisions for impacted workers.
519 a) An employee shall be protected from termination, disciplinary action, retaliation,
520 or other adverse employment action for refusing to follow the output of an artificial intelligence
521 system, automated decision system, algorithm, or other similar technology if the following
522 conditions are met:
523 i) The employee holds independent judgment and discretion in executing their work
524 duties, or the work duties to be performed by the employee require licensure or certification by
525 the commonwealth as a condition of employment, independent accreditation by the employer;
526 ii) The employee has notified a supervisor, manager, or their employer that the
527 output from the artificial intelligence system, automated decision system, algorithm, or other
26 of 32

528 similar technology may, in the employee's professional opinion and/or educational or work
529 related- experience, lead to harm of a natural person, damage to physical property, an illegal
530 action, an action contrary to the licensure or certification requirements of the Federal
531 government, commonwealth, or an applicable private licensing or certifying authority, or an
532 outcome contrary to the goal of the employer, and the employer refused or otherwise failed to
533 adjust the output;
534 iii) The employee has refused to follow the output in good faith and with the
535 knowledge or reasonable belief, based upon training, education, or experience, that the output
536 would cause harm or have an adverse impact; and
537 iv) Due to the urgency of the potential harm or adverse impact, there is not enough
538 time for the output to be corrected through department action.
